# About me, the long version

**TLDR:** I am a software engineer 🧑‍💻 turned product manager 🤹 on a career break ⏸️ to reset, get back to my geeky roots, and restock on some basic knowledge.

In my mind, it's the best time (probably in history) to truly and consciously dedicate focused time to learning. Why? Because AI breakthroughs are equivalent to vaccines, cars, space travel, and personal computers. AI is the thing that will significantly change our world, it's happening at an insanely expedited pace, and we are lucky to witness it!

## Growing up with computers

As a millennial, I belong to one of the fortunate generations that had a childhood mostly free of electronics and became completely dependent on them as young adults (who remembers printing MapQuest directions? ✋).

🌱 I was 7 years old when I got to play my first computer game.  My mom took me and my brother to her office and introduced us to PacMan, Tetris, [Sokoban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sokoban) (my favorite), [Alley Cat](https://youtu.be/jK9kfhOJ9uA?si=wycdKdjj7LmSVh-F), and [Nightmare on Elm's street](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CLsmjwCmm-o)(too scary for me, though I really enjoyed finding the house with lights on!) But I digress... PCs were slowly integrated into our lives, and I grew up with the evolution: first computer game at 7, first AOL account at 13, first programming class at 18 (along with my dad's advice "just google 'bubble sort alg' and get some sleep!" when I ambitiously took six classes in my first semester of college - I hope my comp science teacher doesn't read this and revert that A ;). Having both parents as software engineers likely helped me feel that the computer evolution was naturally happening to make life more convenient one 'byte' at a time 🍽️ (I had to go there! I promise no more corny jokes!).

I have a different sense from AI: I'm curious and eager to keep up, but it almost seems like a full-time job. When I first decided to learn, I was overwhelmed: LLMs, RAG, MCPs etc. So many acronyms. I kept asking my LLM to draw me an AI landscape to find some thread to follow, some starting place beyond switching completely to LLMs for all my search needs and random questions (oh, the 5-year-old in all of us is just having a field day right now!). 

But I digress, this is about me, not about my [AI journey](#), which deserves its own space.

## How I ended up in software

A software engineer by training, I will come clean: I never intended to stay there. I went into software because I needed to pick a major and I was getting a little tired of being left out of my family dinner conversations. Between my SAP Consultant and IT Manager mom, Software Architect dad, and my freshly baked Software Engineer brother, you can imagine the conversations became a bit hard to follow for a high school kid 😉. Since I didn't feel particularly strongly about any major, I said "why not? It seems interesting enough!"

I was surprised to find that I really enjoyed it. Even more so, I enjoyed thinking through software design and optimizing it. Not at an algorithm level (I can appreciate it and I love some elegant code, but my brain does not work this way), but at the big picture: defining the software components and determining how the different pieces should work together. Hold that thought, we will come back to it.

What was even more surprising was that I found my passion after I thought I was well set on the rails of becoming a software engineer / business analyst blend of some sort.

After completing my undergrad, I started the MSIS program at the same school. I chose bioinformatics as the topic for my thesis and, lo and behold, I fell in love! I fell in love enough to come to my first real-life crossroads. Do I...

1. Continue down the path of MSIS, knowing FULL well that, although I love software engineering, I don't love it enough to fully commit to the technical track.
2. Forget everything and go for a PhD in Genetics? I strongly considered it, and, amazingly, at 20 (I graduated early) it felt like it was too late to re-commit to a whole different thing and leave my undergrad behind (I know, I know, the older me totally wants to have a talk with that 20-year-old).
3. Drop out of MSIS and look for a job in a genetics company?

I picked #3. I am forever grateful to DNA Direct leadership for taking a chance on me: a fresh college grad with barely a year of experience to run IT for a genetic testing startup. DNA Direct gave me my baseline foundation of the practical impact genetics can have on our health. It's where I learned about Pharmacogenomics, a topic that still greatly inspires me. It was also sink or swim: as the only tech person in the company, I had to quickly learn how to manage vendors, understand networking, software architecture, website analytics, engineering, deployment, release management and, not least of all, technical Product Management. As the company grew, I was fortunate to shift my focus to discovery and software requirements and everything in-between that Product Managers love so much about their jobs.

## The question that took me fifteen years to answer

My journey to Product Management was long in part due to my curiosity. One of the biggest challenges for DNA Direct, as a direct-to-consumer genetic testing company in the early 2000s, was that there was zero trust in these companies from patients, and full trust in traditional healthcare. And yet it was hard to find a doctor who would agree to do genetic testing. It just wasn't standard of care.

I was building the Pharmacogenomics educational website at the same time as I was taking my grandpa for his daily blood draws to assess the effectiveness of warfarin therapy, a blood thinning drug. The reason my grandpa's doctor gave for daily testing was that "people absorb the drug differently." What I learned from DNA Direct was that warfarin is metabolized through the CYP2C9 pathway, and that poor metabolizers are at a high risk of bleeding, while rapid metabolizers will have almost no effect from the same dose. In my mind, there was a disconnect: why was his dose found by trial and error when a genetic test could have given his doctor a better starting point?

After DNA Direct went through a series of acquisitions and was entirely absorbed by pharma, rather than searching for my next opportunity in Product Management, I was determined to peek under the hood of traditional healthcare to get a better understanding of the other side: why aren't genetic tests becoming standard of care? My way into that world turned out to be interoperability, as I landed a job at a SaaS / Hardware provider focused entirely on facilitating the data transfer between health organizations. Through my interoperability knowledge I joined Tenet and then Valley Medical Center, and got to observe traditional healthcare from inside the care system.

I didn't find one answer. I found layers: best practices, regulatory requirements, and clinicians with no spare hours to adopt anything new. Fifteen years after DNA Direct, I joined Natera. By this time, sequencing had improved, trust had grown, and doctors believed the tests belonged in patient care.

## Natera

At this point, I had reached every aspect of my long dream: officially a product manager at a genetic testing company, at a time when these tests were truly making an impact on patients. I joined right before a period of hypergrowth as an IC Technical Product Manager to launch Prospera. Having been focused on germline testing in my DNA Direct days, I loved learning about cfDNA diagnostic testing. It was an entirely different side of a science I already loved. That success, and my previous experience in team leadership, earned me the trust to lead Technical Product Management for the Austin lab expansion, which gave me a full view of how Natera's internal systems were used and the natural challenges the company faced as it started to grow rapidly.

I saw some of the same challenges I solved at DNA Direct, like the complexity of patient report content and customization, and some new ones: optimizing operational workflows through software to scale. I was inspired by these challenges beyond continuing to update the software to enable new products, so I asked to lead the new team and the vision for internal platform transformation. And that's the thing I told you to hold onto: defining the components and figuring out how the pieces should work together was the job. I found my home, using years of experience in systems, interoperability, and healthcare.

I focused on my passion for five years, heads down, building the proverbial plane while flying. I've heard this expression many times before, but boy did it relate to this situation more than anything else! We continued to launch new products, the company grew from 500 to 4000+ employees, and AI became a major part of the technical stack. To say it very lightly: my roadmap had to be flexible and adaptable.

## Burnout

And here's the truth I will admit: I got burned out. I was 200% invested in the thing I was building, keeping up with all the changes, and I had no time for my growing kids, let alone learning AI beyond using LLMs as my new search engine. I'm grateful to the recent acceptance of burnout as a thing. A friend told me "don't burn out" in my early days at Natera as she saw how I jumped head first into everything. Back then, I laughed and told her I don't believe in burnout. And here I am.

It's not what I thought it would be. I did not suddenly become unable to do anything at all. I carried on, but my passion dwindled. I was surprised that I did not feel compelled to jump back into work after I put my kids to bed as I once did. I did not take my weekends to experiment with Claude Code. I started to second-guess every PRD and every email. I became a 60% version of me, and I was not happy with what I saw.

## So here I am

I'm taking a career break to learn and recharge. For the first part of my career, I was on a mission to find the answer to my question and to grow professionally - no breaks, no time to reflect.  I am taking that time now. There are huge opportunities for AI in everything. I continue to deeply care about improving healthcare through software that improves providers' workflow and access to the newest technologies. I have a few ideas brewing in that department, and I hope to apply them to my next role.

In the meantime, I will tweak, learn, take time for myself, design and build shelving, cook with my kids, weight train, join Toastmasters, run an executive communication group, and volunteer as a program manager for a social community of women in tech. It's a busy schedule, but I no longer second-guess myself. I am full of ideas and energy to make them happen!
